Rethinking video autoencoders for interactive world models

Anonymous authors, under review for ICLR 2027

Drag the divider on any video to compare the real future with the generated one.

Video world models generate in the latent space of an autoencoder. Its compression sets the cost of every frame and how long each action waits to take effect. Yet world models often reuse the autoencoders of text-to-video generators, built for offline generation with no agent waiting. We built CADRE, an open family of causal diffusion representation autoencoders that covers seven spatio-temporal compressions, and trained the same world model on each in two domains, manipulation and navigation, with two environments each. CADRE forms most of the latency-quality frontier, especially with a moving camera.

Below are rollouts in the order of the paper. Each one starts from 2 s of real video. The world model then generates 4.8 s from the recorded actions alone, at 20 frames per second. Blue marks generated video, ochre real video.

§2

A latent frame waits for all its actions

The autoencoder compresses a clip by τ in time and s in space. A latent frame covers τ video frames, so the world model can predict it only once all τ actions are known. The first action of each chunk waits τ/f, then one world-model call and one decode. At τ = 8 and 20 fps the wait alone is 400 ms, beyond the 230 ms lag humans notice in their own movement.

§4

Four environments in two domains

Manipulation, by a robot and by human hands, has a still or nearly still camera. Navigation, driving and indoor, has a moving camera. Each rollout comes from the CADRE member with the best FVD in its environment, decoded in two steps. The first clip in each environment is the one in the paper's Figure 5, whose members generate 29 to 58 frames per second on one H100, more than the 20 they display. For robot manipulation and driving that figure uses 4×16×16; the 2×16×16 shown here reaches 14 frames per second at the same settings.

§4.1

A compressed latent needs a diffusion decoder

A strongly compressed latent fits many videos. MIRA's perceptual regression decoder returns their blurry average. CADRE's diffusion decoder samples one sharp video, in one or two steps thanks to self-distillation. Both compress by 2×32×32. MIRA scores the higher PSNR but the worse FID and FVD in every environment, a sign of blur. Drag the divider to compare them.

Decode a compressed latent by diffusion. In one step it runs as fast as regression, with 1.4 to 2.5× lower FVD and similar action following. A second step helps further.
§4.2

Off-the-shelf autoencoders break when the camera moves

The world model is identical in every panel. Only the latent changes. With a moving camera, Wan 2.2 and LTX-2 dissolve into texture, Cosmos leaves ghosts and MIRA blurs. With a still camera most of them look alike. Under each panel are that autoencoder's FVD in this environment and its action latency on one H100.

CADRE forms most of the latency-quality frontier, especially with a moving camera. In navigation, all CADRE members but one beat every other autoencoder on FID and FVD, while Wan 2.2's FVD grows from 53 in robot manipulation to 600 or more.
App. J

One context, several futures

Each rollout starts from the same real context, then follows either the recorded actions or a sequence never recorded with that context: no motion, another clip's actions, or a constant left or right turn. The futures split when the context ends.

§5.1

Compress time in the autoencoder, not the world model

Both routes reach the same token grid. The autoencoder route is a CADRE member that packs τ frames into each latent. The world-model route keeps the per-frame latent of CADRE-1×16×16 and patches it by τ inside the world model. Action following barely changes, except at 8 frames, where patching drops ARR from 0.72 to 0.56. Switch on the error map to see where each rollout departs from the real robot.

Let the autoencoder compress temporally. At the same number of tokens, its frames look sharper and move more realistically, with similar action following. It learns its compression to reconstruct pixels, while the world model keeps only what helps it predict.
§5.2

Long latents cost little with a still camera and degrade motion with a moving one

Every rollout here uses the same fine token, s = 16, and only the number of frames per latent changes. The plot tracks how much each generated frame differs from the previous one. The ticks mark chunk boundaries for the τ you point at. As τ grows, the change piles up on those boundaries and leaves seams in every environment. With a moving camera they also show in FVD, which rises by up to 64% over two-frame latents.

Manipulation and navigation call for opposite compressions. With a still camera each token sees temporally redundant content, so temporal compression costs little, but spatial compression loses the small objects being handled. With a moving camera each token sees new content, so temporal compression shows seams, but spatial compression costs little because the whole view shifts.
§5.3

Coarser tokens keep frames sharp but lose the motion

Both rollouts pack the same number of frames per latent. The coarse one also compresses more in space. Each frame is just as sharp, but the arms drift from where the actions put them. FVD roughly doubles and action following drops, while FID moves far less.

Surprisingly, coarser tokens hurt motion and action following far more than image quality, above all in manipulation. There the action happens in a small region that stays in view. In navigation, where the whole view shifts, they cost little.
App. H

Sixty-four seconds of robot manipulation

This uses the same protocol, run for 1280 generated frames with a one-step decode. No rollout collapses, but they drift at different rates: FVD grows by 1.5 per second for CADRE-2×16×16, 2.0 for Cosmos, 2.4 for MIRA and 14.5 for Wan 2.2. Wan 2.2 starts near the reconstruction regime, but its colours saturate after about half a minute. MIRA's blur grows into dark blobs. Under each panel is its FVD at 64 s.
